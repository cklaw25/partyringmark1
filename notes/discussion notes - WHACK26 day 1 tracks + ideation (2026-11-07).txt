## "Technically real underneath"

**Analogy:** a film set versus a real house. From the front, both look like a house. Open the door of the film-set one and there's nothing behind it. Judges who code will try the door.

**Fake (a "wrapper"):** the app sends your text to ChatGPT and shows whatever comes back. Nice UI, but **the only "work" is someone else's AI**. If a judge asks "what did *you* build?", the honest answer is "a text box".

**Real:** something you built actually does the work, and the AI, if there is any, is one part of it. Examples:
- Pipeline of Peril (Marshall Wace winner): the servo *physically* tapped out Morse code and a camera *read* QR codes. Silly idea, but every step genuinely worked.
- Your own projects: the cup-fill detection in WaterMeasureMark1 does real signal processing, and UltrasoundMark1 trains an RL agent. That's real.
- If you use an LLM, wrap real logic around it: your own scoring maths, a simulation, live sensor data, a dataset you processed.

**The test:** if you swapped ChatGPT out tomorrow, would most of your project still exist? If yes, it's real.

These judges have built order books and RL drones, so they'll ask **"how does it work?"** You need a satisfying answer to that question, ideally one sentence plus a peek at the code or the hardware.

## "Low latency"

**Latency = delay**: the time between something happening and your system reacting to it.

**Analogy:** two people at an auction. One is in the room and raises a paddle instantly. The other bids by email. The one in the room wins even if both decided at the same moment.

**Numbers, to get a sense of scale:**
| Thing | Rough delay |
|---|---|
| Blink of an eye | 100–400 ms (milliseconds) |
| A typical website loading | 200–1000 ms |
| A ChatGPT reply starting | 500–2000 ms |
| Ping London → New York | ~70 ms |
| A fast trading system reacting to a price change | **a few microseconds** (1 µs = one millionth of a second) |
| Light travelling 300 m | 1 µs |

So "low latency" in trading means **reacting about 100,000× faster than you can blink**.

**Why trading firms care:** when a price moves, the first firm to react gets the good trade and everyone after gets a worse price. Being 1 µs faster is literally money. That's why firms put their servers inside the exchange's building.

**What low-latency engineers actually do:**
- Write C++ or Rust instead of Python, because Python is roughly 50–100× slower.
- Avoid anything that pauses the program unpredictably: memory allocation, locks, garbage collection.
- Make data fit the CPU cache, and sometimes use special network cards or FPGAs (programmable chips).
- **Order book / matching engine** (what Edward and Jakub both built) is the core of an exchange. It keeps the list of buy and sell orders and matches them, and it has to do that in microseconds.

**What it means for you this weekend:** you don't need to build a low-latency system. But if your project has a *speed* angle, those judges will be genuinely interested, especially when you show the measured number ("it reacts in 3 ms"). An ESP32 reacting to a sensor in real time, or a live simulation that updates instantly, would both count.

## What "technically real" means
**A wrapper** is an app where ChatGPT does all the work. You send it text, it sends text back, and you display it. Take the AI away and nothing is left.

**Technically real** means *your own code* does the important work. The AI is one helper part, like the voice or the writer, and it isn't the brain.

**A quick test:** a judge asks "how does it work?" If your honest answer is "we send it to GPT", that's a wrapper. If your answer explains *your* logic, it's real.

**In one line:** incident.io helps tech companies deal with it when their app or website breaks.

## Analogy: a fire brigade for software
Say Deliveroo's app crashes at 7pm on a Friday. Nobody can order, and they lose thousands of pounds a minute. Someone has to:
1. **Notice** it's broken,
2. **Wake up** the right engineer, even at 3am,
3. **Gather** everyone in one place to fix it,
4. **Tell customers** "we know, we're fixing it",
5. **Write up** afterwards what went wrong so it doesn't happen again.

That whole mess is called an **incident**, and incident.io is the software that runs it.

## What the product actually does
| Step | incident.io feature |
|---|---|
| Something breaks | **Alerts** come in from monitoring tools ("server down!", "error rate is 40%") |
| Wake the right person | **On-call paging**: phones the engineer whose turn it is that week |
| Fix it together | Automatically opens a **Slack channel** for the incident, assigns roles (who leads, who talks to customers) and keeps a timeline |
| Tell customers | **Status page**, e.g. "Payments are slow, fix in progress" |
| Learn from it | **Post-mortem**: a report of what happened and why |
| Their new AI part | **AI SRE**: an AI that investigates *while the incident is happening*, reading logs and recent code changes and saying "this probably broke because of Tuesday's update" |

(**SRE** = Site Reliability Engineer, the person whose job is keeping the website up.)

## Who uses it
Netflix, Linear, Etsy, Ramp, and reportedly OpenAI. It's a London startup founded by ex-Monzo engineers. Their main rival is PagerDuty.

## How this connects to their prompt
Their whole business is about **reacting in real time**: when something breaks, every second counts, and asking an AI afterwards is too late. That's why their problem statement says *"the world doesn't wait… build an agent that reacts to a live feed as it happens."*
